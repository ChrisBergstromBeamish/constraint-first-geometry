The Separation Certificate

If "independence is not free," there has to be a way to check whether a specific proposed separation has actually been paid for. This page gives the public form of that check: a fixed set of questions a proposed separation must answer before it is treated as physically real rather than merely assumed.

Status: the Separation Certificate is a checklist derived from the program's foundational constraints, restated here at a level suitable for a general audience. It is a tool for classifying claims, not itself a new physical law. Applying it to a specific claim can move that claim into a recognizable pattern — see Kinds of Separation — but the certificate does not, by itself, prove or disprove anything.

The seven questions

A proposed separation — of a system into subsystems, a geometry into a product of pieces, a spectrum into independent sectors, an observer from an observed system — is asked to answer all seven of the following. An honest answer of "not yet" to any one of them means the separation is not yet established; it is a candidate, not a fact.

The questions draw on two different layers of the program's checks: the core consistency principles (Shape, Scale, Granularity, Invariance, Nonseparability) that any claim in the theory has to satisfy, plus one additional audit question — causal order — that is asked specifically of a proposed separation, to catch the case where a split was chosen only after the result it explains was already known.

CheckQuestion the separation must answer
Shape What object is being split? Is this split part of the same frozen object — the same branch, the same version — or did the argument silently switch to a different object partway through?
Shape (cont.) Is the split described precisely enough to be checked by someone else, rather than gestured at?
Scale Does the split introduce a new scale, threshold, or normalization that was not already accounted for? A hidden second scale is a hidden second assumption, not a free description.
Granularity What finite record proves the split exists? A distinction that cannot terminate in a finite, auditable observation is not yet a physical distinction.
Invariance Does the split survive the transformations it is supposed to survive — change of coordinates, gauge, basis, representation, or convention? A split that only exists in one convention is an artifact of the convention, not of the physics.
Causal order (separation-specific audit question, not one of the core five) Was the split fixed before the answer it is being used to explain was known? A split chosen after seeing the target result is target-fitting, not a genuine structural finding.
Nonseparability Is the independence of the two sides — the claim that they really are separate — proven, paid for by an observed anchor, finitely recorded, or is it still an open assumption?

How to read a "no" answer

None of these questions is designed to be a trap that kills every separation. Ordinary, well-established separations in physics — two detectors at different locations, two decay channels with different final states, two particles with different measured masses — answer all seven questions easily, because they are backed by finite, invariant, causally-ordered, already-paid-for observations. The certificate is not skeptical of separation in general. It is skeptical of separations that are assumed for convenience and never actually checked.

A "no" or "not yet" on one question does not mean the separation is false. It means the separation's status is currently open, and the honest thing to do is say so, rather than write the split into a theory as though it were settled.

The one-sentence version

For a general reader

A thing is separate only when the theory can say what separates it, at what scale, by what finite record — and can show that split is not simply a hidden assumption dressed up as a description.

Where this has actually been applied

Two concrete places in the tested gate work have run something like this certificate against a real structural claim, with named results:

  • Whether the internal geometry's product structure (three separate geometric factors, one per gauge group) is free or has to be paid for. See The Standard Model as Interface.
  • Whether additions to the Standard Model's particle content are legitimate or unpaid "spectator" sectors. See Kinds of Separation.

Both are named on this site with their actual, current, unrounded status — including what is still open.
