Two ways to compute the universe — one 13.8-billion-year answer

Ask two methods that share no machinery what time it is in the universe — standard hot-Big-Bang cosmology, and this framework’s granularity method, which rebuilds the whole timeline from the cost of holding a record — and both land on the same 13.8-billion-year-old universe today. Checked blind, milestone by milestone, across 44 tests: 30 confirmed agreements, 0 disagreements — and the 14 indeterminate split cleanly into 6 standing bets where both routes already agree and the experiment hasn’t weighed in yet, plus 8 questions that are open for all of cosmology. The whole denominator, always: 44 tests / 30 agree / 14 indeterminate / 0 disagree.

The discipline, before the numbers: every framework number is computed first and compared after — no observed value is loaded before the framework’s own answer is written down, and no number is tuned to match data after the fact. Where a row consumes a shared measured input, the row says so on its face; where a question is open, both methods are shown reaching the same honest limit, never a manufactured match. Observing means paying for a record

To observe something is to make a record of a distinction — this here, not that; a particle resolved from the background. A record is not free: it must be paid for out of the granularity available at that moment, and the cost runs one way. The cost of a record grows as you push back toward the Big Bang; it is cheapest — and the affordable distinctions finest — here in the present. The universe’s history runs over all time; it does not switch on at the Big Bang. What switches on is affordability: only where the granularity budget can pay for a record is anything actually knowable.

Because the cost of a record is time-dependent, granularity opens a window of observability — a span of time within which distinctions can be afforded and therefore recorded. Outside that window there is no contradiction and no edge of time; there is simply nothing that could be written down.

The Big Bang: the first payable record

In this reading the Big Bang is not the beginning of time. It is the beginning of the knowable — the first moment at which a record of any distinction could be paid for. That dissolves the oldest question in cosmology without a singularity. “Before the Big Bang” is not an earlier clock reading waiting to be discovered; it is the absence of any record for time to be measured against — like asking what is north of the North Pole. Nothing blows up, and nothing needs to. Both roads stop at the identical Planck wall, t ≈ 5.39×10⁻⁴⁴ s, and this reading turns that wall from a dead end into a definite statement about where recordable history begins. Dissolved, not solved — the reframing removes the question rather than answering it with new data.

The question “what came before the Big Bang?” has an answer here: the epoch where no distinction could yet be recorded. Not an earlier time — an unpayable one.

What the two methods compute — and where they meet

With a time-dependent granularity in hand, the entire cosmic timeline becomes computable a second way: read off when each kind of record first becomes payable, then lay that answer beside the standard hot-Big-Bang calculation of the same milestone. The two roads meet, with numbers:

Quark confinement — ~20 microseconds

Free color charge stops being a recordable distinction at T_c ≈ 156.5 MeV — the same confinement point from both routes.

Neutrino decoupling — ~1 second

Both routes return the famously fractional Neff = 3.044, against Planck’s measured 2.99 ± 0.17.

The first nuclei — ~3 minutes

Deuterium and helium-4 abundances land within 0.2–0.7σ of measurement, from a single baryon count consistent with the CMB.

The CMB released — 380,000 years

Both roads put last scattering at the identical redshift, z* = 1089.92 — Planck measures 1089.92 ± 0.25.

…and onward to the same 13.8-billion-year age today — the master clock check that opens the scoreboard.

Agreement is the argument

The granularity method and standard cosmology share no machinery — no common equations, no common calibration, no shared free parameters. When both, run independently, land on the same number for when a milestone occurs, that agreement cannot be an artifact of one feeding the other. Across 44 checkpoints it happens 30 times, with 0 disagreements — and a single genuine disagreement would count against the whole construction. The test can fail; so far it hasn’t.

The 14 indeterminate rows are the discipline that makes the 30 believable, and they split cleanly in two. Six are standing bets — both routes already agree and the experiment hasn’t weighed in yet: LiteBIRD’s tensor band, the disputed 21-cm trough depth, the isocurvature null, the axion window, non-Gaussianity, the reheating window. Eight are open for all of cosmology — baryogenesis, dark-matter identity, lithium-7, the S8 clumpiness tension, the primordial magnetic field, the Planck wall itself. None is rounded up; every row shows the whole denominator. And where an agreement runs through machinery both roads necessarily share — the standard nuclear network, the standard growth equations — the row is marked as a consistency check, on its face.

The knowable beginning and the knowable end

One structural feature falls out of reading the beginning this way: the knowable beginning and the knowable end are always the same distance in time from us. We never actually reach the end. As the knowable beginning recedes further into the past, the knowable end recedes by exactly the same amount into the future — the knowable universe is a window that widens symmetrically about us, never a wall we arrive at. It is a consequence of granularity being time-dependent, not an assumption bolted on afterward. Where this record-and-cost vocabulary is pointed past the certified physics — at experience, at how separate things emerge from one geometry — is Horizons, and it is labeled interpretive there in the open.
