A second, independent way to read the early universe

The genuinely new thing in this section is a method. The theory carries a granularity — the finite cost of laying down a single distinction, a single record — and that cost changes with time. That gives us a second, fully independent way to do ordinary early-universe calculations, run alongside standard cosmology and then checked against it. When two methods that share no machinery land on the same number, the agreement itself is the argument.

Read this before anything else here: nothing in this section is a confirmed prediction in the reserved sense. What follows is a way of computing the cosmic timeline twice — once from the granularity budget, once from standard cosmology — and reporting, milestone by milestone, whether the two agree. No number is tuned to match data after the fact. Where a milestone is still open, both methods are shown reaching the same honest limit, not a manufactured match.

Observing means paying for a record

To observe something is to make a record of a distinction — this here, not that; a particle resolved from the background. A record is not free: it must be paid for out of the granularity available at that moment. This reframes the whole question of "the beginning." The universe's history runs over all time, from the far past to the far future; it does not switch on at the Big Bang. What switches on is affordability. Only where the granularity budget can pay for a record is anything actually knowable.

Because the cost of a record is time-dependent, granularity opens a window of observability — a span of time within which distinctions can be afforded and therefore recorded. Outside that window there is no contradiction and no edge of time; there is simply nothing that could be written down.

The Big Bang as the beginning of the knowable

In this reading the Big Bang is not the beginning of time. It is the beginning of the knowable — the first moment at which a record of any distinction could be paid for. This dissolves the old puzzle of "what came before the Big Bang" without a singularity. There is a "before"; it is simply before the knowable, the epoch in which no record could yet be afforded. "Before" is not an earlier clock reading waiting to be discovered — it is the absence of affordable records. Nothing blows up, and nothing needs to.

The question "what came before the Big Bang?" has an answer here: the epoch where no distinction could yet be recorded. Not an earlier time — an unpayable one.

What we actually compute

With a time-dependent granularity in hand, two concrete quantities become calculable from first principles:

When observation can first begin

The earliest moment at which the granularity budget can pay for a single record — the opening edge of the window of observability. We compute this from the budget alone, with no cosmological input.

When individual particles become resolvable

The moment each species can first be told apart from its background and recorded as a distinct thing. Again computed from granularity — then the same milestone is computed the standard cosmological way, and the two are compared.

The discipline is the same one that governs the rest of this site: compute natively first, compare after, and report the honest status. The full pipeline is described in the blind prediction method.

Agreement is the argument

The granularity method and standard cosmology share no machinery — no common equations, no common calibration, no shared free parameters. So when both, run independently, land on the same number for when a milestone occurs, that agreement cannot be an artifact of one feeding the other. Repeated across dozens of milestones, this becomes a strong, non-circular argument for the construction. A single genuine disagreement would count against it — the test can fail.

The current scoreboard, checked blind: 44 milestones across the cosmic timeline — 36 agree with standard cosmology, 0 disagree, and 8 are open. The 8 open ones are not private failures of this method; they are the questions the whole field still shares, and on those both methods are shown reaching the same honest limit rather than a fabricated match.

The knowable beginning and the knowable end

One structural feature falls out of reading the beginning this way: the knowable beginning and the knowable end are always the same distance in time from us. We never actually reach the end. As the knowable beginning recedes further into the past, the knowable end recedes by exactly the same amount into the future — the knowable universe is a window that widens symmetrically about us, never a wall we arrive at. It is a consequence of granularity being time-dependent, not an assumption bolted on afterward.
